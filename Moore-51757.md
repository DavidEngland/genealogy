== Biography ==
'''Elizabeth E. Moore''' (c. 1740–c. 1780-1782) was born on March 19, 1740, in the Indian Creek area of Tryon County (now Lincoln County), North Carolina. Her parentage remains unresolved (see Research Notes below), but she was definitely part of the prominent Moore family of Loyalists in that region. She married '''Major Nicholas Welch''' (c. 1738–1814) in 1757 in North Carolina. Elizabeth died during the chaotic period of exile following the Patriot victories at the Battle of Ramsour's Mill and King's Mountain, likely perishing between 1780-1782 in North Carolina, Georgia, or early East Florida.

=== Marriage and Family ===

Elizabeth married '''[[Welch-185|Nicholas Welch]]''', a colonial Pennsylvania landowner who later became a controversial Loyalist officer during the American Revolution.

=== Life and Migration ===

Elizabeth's life spanned a transformative and tragic period in American history. Born in colonial North Carolina, she married during the imperial era and witnessed the American Revolution as the wife of a Loyalist officer. 

'''Death During Exile (c. 1780-1782):'''

Following the Patriot victory at the Battle of Ramsour's Mill (June 20, 1780) and King's Mountain (October 7, 1780), Elizabeth and her family were banished from North Carolina by Revolutionary authorities. The family was "stript of every thing they had, except only a small part of their wearing apparel" and sent as refugees into Georgia.

'''Critical Documentary Evidence:''' East Florida Claims Commission testimony from 1782 describes '''Nicholas Welsh''' arriving at '''St. Johns Town, East Florida''' "'''as a widower with six children'''." This definitive contemporary evidence establishes that Elizabeth died sometime between June 1780 (the Battle of Ramsour's Mill) and 1782 (the family's arrival in East Florida).

The circumstances of her death remain unknown, but she likely perished during:
* The forced exile from North Carolina (1780)
* The refugee period in Georgia (1780-1782)
* The migration to East Florida (1782)

The hardships of displacement, loss of property, separation from family support networks, and the privations of refugee life took a devastating toll on Loyalist families. Elizabeth was one of countless victims of the civil war aspect of the American Revolution.

'''Her Descendants' Later Migration:'''

Though Elizabeth did not survive to see it, her descendants became prominent settlers in the Tennessee-Alabama frontier, including War of 1812 veterans, farmers, and frontier families who established communities in Hardin County, Tennessee, and surrounding regions. Her widowed husband Nicholas eventually returned to the United States and settled in Giles County, Tennessee, where he died in 1814.

=== Revolutionary War and the Battle of Ramsour's Mill (1780) ===

'''The Moore Family and Loyalist Leadership:'''

Elizabeth belonged to the prominent Moore family of Tryon County (now Lincoln County), North Carolina. '''Captain Moses Moore''' (1718–1797), a likely kinsman, resided on Indian Creek approximately eight miles west of present-day Lincolnton and roughly seven miles from Jacob Ramsour's Mill. The Moore family home became the organizational center for Loyalist mobilization in the region.

'''Lt. Colonel John "The Tory" Moore''' (1753–1781), likely Elizabeth's brother or close kinsman, commanded the Loyalist forces in '''Hamilton's Tory Regiment''' and served as the primary commander at the Battle of Ramsour's Mill. Elizabeth's husband, '''Major Nicholas Welch''', served as a Loyalist/Tory officer in the same regiment, residing "just above Moore on Indian Creek."

'''Loyalist Mobilization (June 1780):'''

On June 7, 1780, Welch and John Moore returned to the Moses Moore residence on Indian Creek to begin organizing local residents for the British cause. The '''first meeting of Loyalist recruits took place at the Moses Moore residence on June 10, 1780''', making the Moore family home the launching point for the ill-fated Loyalist campaign.

'''The Battle of Ramsour's Mill''' (June 20, 1780) was a brutal engagement between Loyalist and Patriot neighbors. The battle took place on a wooded hill about 300 yards east of the gristmill owned by Derick Ramsour (also spelled "Ramseur"), located on the west bank of Clark's Creek in what is now Lincolnton, Lincoln County, North Carolina.

'''Modern Location:''' The battlefield site is located on the north side of Lincolnton. '''Battleground Elementary School and Lincolnton High School''' are situated directly upon the ground where the fighting occurred, preserving the historical significance of the site through their names.

Moore and Welch had been actively recruiting Loyalists to support British General Cornwallis's advance into North Carolina. The Patriot victory at Ramsour's Mill became the '''first domino''' in a series of defeats that culminated in the decisive British defeat at '''King's Mountain''' (October 7, 1780), which turned the tide of the Southern Campaign.

'''Consequences and Exile:'''

Following King's Mountain, Elizabeth and her Loyalist husband faced severe consequences from Patriot forces. As the family of a Loyalist officer, Elizabeth, '''Major Nicholas Welch''', and their '''nine children''' were banished from North Carolina by Revolutionary authorities.

'''Exile Route (for surviving family):'''

* Forced departure from Lincoln/Tryon County region, North Carolina (1780)
* Relocation to Georgia, then East Florida (1780-1782)
* '''Elizabeth's death''' occurred during this period (c. 1780-1782)
* Nicholas and surviving children: Settlement in '''New Providence, Bahamas''' (1784)

After Elizabeth's death, Nicholas continued as a widower, petitioning the British government for compensation for their confiscated 600-acre North Carolina estate. Nicholas remained in the Bahamas and London until at least March 1786, when documented petitions show him seeking restitution from the Crown.

=== Separation of the Family: The Bahamas Branch ===

The Welch family experienced a permanent split during the exile period:

'''Those Who Remained in the Islands:'''

* '''[[Welch-15719|Mary Welch]]''' (b. 1762) — Married '''James Misick''' in Nassau, New Providence, in 1785 and remained in the Caribbean, eventually settling in the Turks Islands, where she died in 1822. This Bahamas connection may explain family ties to the Gulf Coast region, as some descendants may have maintained Caribbean-Gulf of Mexico trade routes. There are burial records of family members near New Orleans, Louisiana, suggesting ongoing connections between the island-dwelling branch and the mainland.

'''Those Who Returned to America:'''
Most of the Welch children eventually returned to the American frontier, though the exact timing and sequence of departures from the Bahamas remains unclear in genealogical records. Their mother Elizabeth had already died by this point (c. 1780-1782).

=== Return and Settlement in the American Frontier (1800–1814) ===

By the early 1800s, Major Nicholas Welch and his family strategically relocated from the Caribbean back to the American frontier, following a migration corridor through the Carolinas, Kentucky, and into Tennessee.

'''South Carolina (c. 1800):'''

* Major Nicholas Welch and his son '''[[Welch-1890|Lewis Welch]]''' are recorded in South Carolina around 1800, establishing a foothold in the post-war United States.
* Son '''Nicholas Welch Jr.''' was born in '''Pendleton County, South Carolina''', in 1786, suggesting some family presence there even during the Bahamas exile period.

'''Hopkins County, Kentucky (c. 1806–1810):'''

* The family moved into Kentucky by 1810, with '''Lewis Welch''' recorded living next to '''Henry Welch''' in Hopkins County during this period.
* This represents the family's reestablishment in the westward-moving American frontier after more than a decade of Caribbean exile.

=== Settlement Across Tennessee Counties (1810–1814 and Beyond) ===

After establishing a foothold in Kentucky, Elizabeth and her children dispersed across multiple Tennessee counties, each establishing themselves as prominent settlers and War of 1812 veterans:

'''Giles County (Primary Settlement):'''

* '''Major Nicholas Welch''' (Elizabeth's husband) settled here and appears on the '''1812 tax list'''.
* Elizabeth likely spent her final years here with Nicholas.
* Major Nicholas Welch died in Giles County in '''December 1814'''.
* Daughter '''[[Welch-8497|Sarah Welch]]''' (b. 1766, d. 1826) remained in Giles County and served as the administratrix of her father's estate.
* Other children including '''[[Welch-8499|John Welch]]''' (d. 1814, Giles County) and '''[[Welch-8498|Thomas Welch]]''' (d. 1810, Tennessee) settled here.

'''Lawrence County (1810s–1820s):'''

* Court records from the 1820s (the "Crockett Docket") contain approximately '''50 mentions''' of the Welch surname, indicating strong family presence.
* Son '''Nicholas Welch Jr.''' married '''Elizabeth Maxey''' in '''1819''', establishing the family line in Lawrence County.

'''Perry County (Primary Frontier Hub):'''

* '''Nicholas Welch Jr.''' was one of the first settlers on '''White Oak Creek''' (1818–1820) and subsequently served as '''Sheriff of Perry County''' after 1830.
* '''Lewis Welch''' moved to Perry County by 1820, appearing in the census with his sons, including '''[[Welch-1890|Lewis Welch]]'''.
* '''Zachariah Welch''' settled on the banks of the Tennessee River near the Wayne/Perry line on a '''War of 1812 land grant''', exemplifying the family's prominence as frontier veterans.

'''Hardin County:'''

* Son '''[[Welch-8501|William C. Welch]]''' (b. 1758, d. 1838) settled in Hardin County and is buried in the '''Savannah Cemetery'''.
* Grandson '''John C. Welch''' (son of Lewis) was also a resident of Hardin County.
* This county became home to prominent second-generation descendants, including '''[[Welch-1883|Elijah C. Welch]]''' (Elizabeth's great-grandson), who married '''[[Taylor-2744|Mary Taylor]]''' and whose daughter '''[[Welch-1878|Serena Welch]]''' married into the distinguished '''Hargrove''' family.

'''Wayne County:'''

* Descendant '''Elijah C. Welch''' (grandson of Major Nicholas) lived in Wayne County, maintaining family connections to multiple Tennessee counties.

'''Gibson County:'''

* Sons '''[[Welch-8500|Richard Welch]]''' (b. 1770, d. 1861) and '''James Welch''' settled here.
* Richard Welch left a will in Gibson County in 1861, indicating long-term family establishment.

=== Legacy: From Exile to Frontier Settlement ===

Elizabeth Moore Welch's life trajectory—from colonial North Carolina through Caribbean exile to Tennessee frontier prominence—embodies the complex experience of Loyalist families during and after the American Revolution. While she and her family lost their original estate through confiscation, they successfully reestablished themselves as prominent frontier settlers, War of 1812 veterans, and community leaders across multiple Tennessee and Kentucky counties.

Her sixteen children and subsequent descendants became instrumental in settling the Tennessee River Valley during the early 19th century, transforming from exiled refugees to foundational frontier families. The dispersal of her children across Giles, Lawrence, Perry, Wayne, Hardin, and Gibson counties represents a deliberate frontier settlement strategy, with each son or family group securing land through military service, land grants, and frontier enterprise.

=== Death ===

Elizabeth died circa 1780-1782, most likely during the forced exile from North Carolina or while living as a refugee in Georgia or early East Florida. The exact circumstances, date, and location of her death remain unknown.

'''Documentary Evidence:''' East Florida Claims Commission testimony from 1782 describes her husband Nicholas arriving at St. Johns Town, East Florida "'''as a widower with six children'''," providing definitive contemporary proof that Elizabeth had died by that time.

'''Note on Conflicting Records:''' Find a Grave Memorial #234100609 lists her death as "1799 in Giles County, Tennessee," but this is contradicted by the 1782 widower testimony. The 1799 date appears to be a genealogical error that has propagated through online databases. Elizabeth could not have died in Tennessee in 1799 if her husband was documented as a widower in Florida in 1782.

No burial location has been identified for Elizabeth Moore.

== Research Notes ==

=== Death Date Correction: 1799 Error vs. 1780-1782 Evidence ===

'''Previous Error:''' Elizabeth E. Moore has been widely listed in genealogical databases (including Find A Grave Memorial #234100609) as dying in "1799 in Giles County, Tennessee." This date is '''contradicted by contemporary primary source evidence''' and represents a significant genealogical error that has propagated through online family trees.

'''Primary Source Evidence:''' 

East Florida Claims Commission testimony from 1782 provides definitive contemporary documentation that Nicholas Welch arrived at '''St. Johns Town, East Florida''' "'''as a widower with six children'''."<ref>University of Florida, Florida History Online, "St. Johns Town" (accessed via MelaneyWelch posting, 06 Sep 2013)</ref> This testimony was given to British officials documenting Loyalist refugees' property claims, making it highly reliable.

'''Timeline Establishing Death:'''

* '''June 20, 1780''' — Battle of Ramsour's Mill; Loyalist defeat
* '''October 7, 1780''' — Battle of King's Mountain; decisive Patriot victory
* '''Late 1780''' — Elizabeth and her nine children banished from North Carolina, forced into Georgia as refugees
* '''1780-1782''' — '''Elizabeth's death''' (exact date and circumstances unknown)
* '''1782''' — Nicholas documented as "widower" in St. Johns Town, East Florida
* '''1784''' — Nicholas and surviving children relocate to New Providence, Bahamas
* '''1794''' — Nicholas remarries (Sarah Farren, Philadelphia)
* '''1814''' — Nicholas dies in Giles County, Tennessee (Sarah as widow)

'''Conclusion:''' Elizabeth E. Moore died circa 1780-1782, most likely during the forced exile from North Carolina or while living as a refugee in Georgia or early East Florida. The "1799" date is impossible given the 1782 widower documentation.

=== Conflicting Parentage Information ===

Elizabeth E. Moore's parentage remains '''unresolved''' with multiple conflicting sources. The Moore family connections are confident, but the exact placement within the family tree requires further research:

'''Theory 1: Daughter of Lt. Col. John William Moore and Mary Susannah (Jouett) Moore'''

* '''Find A Grave''' Memorial #234157064 lists her parents as Lt. Col. John William Moore and Mary Susannah (Jouett) Moore
* '''WikiTree [[Moore-1641]]''' lists John William Moore (1706–1777) married to Mary Susannah Jouett, with daughter Elizabeth Moore (b. 1742) - '''but this is a different Elizabeth'''
* '''Conflict:''' WikiTree [[Moore-1640]] shows Elizabeth Moore (b. 1742) as their daughter, not Elizabeth E. Moore (b. 1740)

'''Theory 2: Daughter of Captain Moses Moore (1718–1797) and Hester Winston'''

* '''WikiTree [[Moore-60716]]''' identifies Moses Moore married to '''[[Winston-1414|Hester Winston]]'''
* '''FindAGrave data''' shows CPT Moses Moore (1718–1797) as parent of Captain John Moore, with siblings including:
**LTC John "The Tory" Moore (1753–1781) - commander at Ramsour's Mill
** Hester Moore Roberts (1762–1838)
** Sarah Moore Logan (d. 1840)
* Historical records confirm Moses Moore's residence on Indian Creek, eight miles west of Lincolnton, served as Loyalist mobilization headquarters (June 1780)
* '''Lt. Colonel John Moore''' (1753–1781), commander of Hamilton's Tory Regiment at Ramsour's Mill, fits as Elizabeth's brother in this scenario

'''Theory 3: Moses Moore from Carlisle, England'''

* Some sources identify her father as '''Moses Moore''', native of Carlisle, England, who migrated to Virginia (1745) before settling in North Carolina
* '''Question:''' Is this the same Moses Moore as CPT Moses Moore (1718–1797), or a different individual?

=== Key Questions Requiring Resolution ===

* Elizabeth E. Moore '''is definitely connected to the Moore family''' of Lincoln/Tryon County, North Carolina
