== Biography ==

'''Moses Moore''' (c. 1718–1791/1797) was born circa 1718 in Carlisle, England, and immigrated to America about 1745. He became a prominent landowner and planter in North Carolina, living on Indian Creek in what was then Lincoln County (present-day Gaston County). As a committed Loyalist during the American Revolution, he was the father of the "notorious Tory" '''Colonel John Moore''', whose allegiance to the British Crown made the entire Moore family targets of Patriot persecution. Following the Revolutionary War, Moses pursued a remarkably strategic survival path: rather than following fellow Loyalist [[Welch-185|Major Nicholas Welch]] into maritime exile via the Bahamas and London, Moses moved his household to '''Spanish West Florida''' and secured an 800-acre land grant on the Tombigbee River—a frontier relocation strategy that ultimately proved more successful than seeking Crown compensation.

=== Early Life and Immigration ===

Moses Moore was born circa 1718 in '''Carlisle, England'''. He immigrated to the American colonies around 1745, settling in the North Carolina backcountry during the great wave of immigration that populated the Piedmont region. By the 1750s-1760s, he had established himself as a successful planter on '''Indian Creek''' in what was then '''Tryon County''' (later Lincoln County, now Gaston County), North Carolina.

=== North Carolina Settlement and Family ===

Moses Moore became a substantial landowner and planter in the Indian Creek community. He married '''Hester Winston''' (documented variously as "Hester Moore" after marriage) and established one of the prominent families in the region.

'''Children of Moses Moore and Hester Winston:'''
* '''[[Moore-2747|Colonel John Moore]]''' ("The Tory") - Loyalist officer during the Revolution
* '''Aaron Moore''' - Pioneer and trader
* '''Sarah Moore''' - married Logan
* '''Hester Moore''' - married Roberts
* '''Ann Moore''' - married Joseph Lawrence (later claimed the Sunflower Plantation)

The Moore family were neighbors and close associates of the Welch family. [[Welch-185|Major Nicholas Welch]] lived "just above Moore on Indian Creek," and Nicholas married '''[[Moore-51757|Elizabeth E. Moore]]''' (c. 1740–c. 1780-1782), likely Moses Moore's daughter or niece, cementing the alliance between these two prominent Loyalist families.

=== The Revolutionary War: A Loyalist Household ===

The Moore family's unwavering loyalty to the British Crown during the American Revolution brought catastrophic consequences. Moses's son '''Colonel John Moore''' became one of the most notorious Loyalist officers in North Carolina, actively recruiting for the British and leading Tory militia forces. This made the entire Moore family targets for Patriot confiscation, harassment, and potential execution.

'''The Revolutionary War Split:'''
* Moses Moore and his family remained steadfastly loyal to King George III
* Their neighbor and in-law [[Welch-185|Major Nicholas Welch]] served as a Loyalist Major under Colonel Hamilton
* Both families faced property confiscation and banishment after the Patriot victory
* The families would pursue different post-war survival strategies

=== The Loyalist Diaspora: Diverging Paths (1783-1787) ===

Following the '''Treaty of Paris (1783)''' that ended the Revolutionary War and granted the colonies independence, Loyalist families scattered in what became known as the '''Tory Diaspora'''. The Moore and Welch families, despite their close ties, chose dramatically different paths to survival:

'''Nicholas Welch's Maritime Exile:'''
* Fled to East Florida (1782), then evacuated to the Bahamas (1784)
* Traveled to London (1786) to petition for compensation
* Received Lord Cornwallis's testimony and a £448 award
* Eventually returned to South Carolina (1786-1788) to rebuild through land speculation

'''Moses Moore's Frontier Relocation:'''
* Moved directly to the '''Mobile District''' of '''Spanish West Florida'''
* Strategically pursued land acquisition under the Spanish Crown
* Focused on establishing a new plantation in a region of fluid territorial boundaries
* Avoided the humiliation of petitioning British authorities for compensation

This divergence represented two distinct Loyalist survival strategies: one focused on '''restitution through legal channels''', the other on '''frontier opportunity in contested territories'''.

=== The Spanish West Florida Migration ===

Unlike Nicholas Welch, who fled to the Bahamas and London to seek restitution, Moses Moore moved his household to the '''Mobile District''' of Spanish West Florida to escape persecution as a Loyalist. This move was strategically centered on acquiring land under the Spanish Crown, which had regained control of the Floridas following the '''Treaty of Paris (1783)'''.

'''The Migration Context:'''
* Spain controlled West Florida from 1783 onwards (having recaptured it from Britain during the Revolution)
* The region offered land grants to settlers who would pledge loyalty to Spain
* The Tombigbee River valley was sparsely populated frontier, far from Patriot persecution
* Spanish authorities needed settlers to establish their territorial claims
* The area's political future was uncertain—creating opportunities for strategic land claims

=== The Sunflower Plantation and Spanish Land Grant (1787) ===

Moses Moore's most significant achievement in Spanish West Florida was securing a substantial land grant that would provide security for his family and legal claim under future American sovereignty.

'''The Land Grant Process:'''

'''October 1, 1787''' - Moses Moore petitioned the '''Governor General of Louisiana''' for a tract of land on the '''Tombigbee River'''. This property, approximately '''800 acres''', had previously belonged to a British commissary named '''John McIntosh'''. When Spain recaptured West Florida in 1780, McIntosh evacuated, and the Spanish authorities considered the land vacant and available for grant.

'''October 22, 1787''' - The grant was approved by '''Estevan Miró''' (Spanish Governor), and Moses Moore took possession of what became known as the '''Sunflower Plantation'''.

'''Location and Description:'''
* Western side of the Tombigbee River
* Near the modern-day town of '''Sunflower, Alabama'''
* Approximately '''800 acres''' of prime river bottomland
* Suitable for plantation agriculture with enslaved labor
* Strategic location in the constantly shifting territorial boundaries between Spanish Florida and American territories

'''Shifting Jurisdictions:'''
This region changed hands multiple times, transitioning from British to Spanish rule before being absorbed into the '''Mississippi Territory''' of the United States following the '''Louisiana Purchase (1803)''' and subsequent territorial adjustments under the '''Adams-Onís Treaty (1819)'''.

=== The Value of Spanish Land Grants ===

Moses Moore's foresight in securing a Spanish land grant proved extraordinarily valuable. These grants provided a legal basis for land claims under the American government after the United States acquired the territory. The Spanish documentation—though in a foreign language—was recognized by U.S. authorities as legitimate proof of ownership and occupancy rights.

'''Legal Strategy:'''
* Spanish grants were honored by U.S. territorial authorities
* "Right of occupancy" was recognized even when formal patents were delayed
* The Moore family's 800 acres survived the transition from Spanish to American sovereignty
* The land became valuable Alabama cotton plantation territory in the early 19th century

=== Death and Legacy ===

Moses Moore died between '''1791''' (per "Pioneers of Washington County, Alabama") and '''1797''' (per Find a Grave sources). The exact date and circumstances of his death remain unclear, but he likely died at his Sunflower Plantation on the Tombigbee River.

'''Succession and the 1804 Patent:'''

After Moses's death, ownership of the 800-acre Sunflower Plantation became contested. In 1797, heirs of the original British grantee '''John McIntosh''' attempted to reclaim the property. Multiple hearings were held before American territorial authorities to determine rightful ownership.

'''March 28, 1804''' - '''Ann Lawrence''' (Moses Moore's daughter, married to Joseph Lawrence) successfully obtained a formal U.S. patent for the 800 acres from the '''Board of Commissioners''' based on her father's "'''right of occupancy'''" under the Spanish grant. This legal victory secured the Moore family's claim against the McIntosh heirs and confirmed Moses's strategic decision to pursue Spanish West Florida settlement rather than maritime exile.

=== The Moore and Welch "Party": Dispersed Children ===

The exodus to Spanish West Florida may have included more than just the Moore family. Strong circumstantial evidence suggests that some of Nicholas Welch's children joined the Moore household in the western migration:

'''The Dispersal of Welch Children:'''
* In his '''1786 Memorial''', Nicholas Welch stated that while he was in London, his children were "'''dispersed... among such families as could be prevailed on to take them in'''"
* Moses Moore's migration party included his daughter '''Ann Lawrence''' and her husband '''Joseph Lawrence'''
* The close family ties between the Moores and Welches (through Elizabeth E. Moore's marriage to Nicholas) created natural refuge arrangements

'''Nicholas Welch Jr.'s Path:'''
* [[Welch-11475|Nicholas William Welch Jr.]] (b. 1762), the older son, became the "man of the family" on the mainland
* He did NOT reunite with his father in the Bahamas
* He remained in the '''South Carolina/Georgia/Alabama''' region
* He married '''Margaret Hembree''' and established himself as a frontier planter
* His path closely parallels the Moore family's westward migration

'''Other Welch Sons:'''
* Records show Nicholas Welch's sons '''William''' and '''Thomas''' also remained on the mainland
* They eventually renounced the Crown and took oaths to the Patriot cause in South Carolina
* This occurred while their father was still in exile in the Bahamas and London

=== Two Loyalist Survival Strategies ===

The divergent paths of Moses Moore and Nicholas Welch represent the two primary survival strategies available to defeated Loyalists after the Revolution:

'''Strategy 1: Maritime Exile and Petition (Nicholas Welch)'''
* Flee to British-controlled territories (Bahamas, Nova Scotia, England)
* Petition the Crown for compensation through Loyalist Claims Commission
* Accept relatively modest financial awards (Nicholas received £448 for £1,150 in claims)
* Return to United States with capital to speculate in land markets
* Rely on surveying skills and frontier knowledge

'''Strategy 2: Frontier Relocation (Moses Moore)'''
* Move to contested territories with fluid sovereignty (Spanish Florida, Louisiana Territory)
* Acquire land grants from new sovereign powers (Spain)
* Establish occupancy claims recognized by successive governments
* Position oneself in regions certain to be absorbed by expanding United States
* Profit from transition as territories become American cotton country

'''The Outcome:'''

Moses Moore's strategy proved remarkably successful. By 1804, his heirs held clear title to 800 prime Alabama acres. The Tombigbee region where British and Spanish boundaries constantly shifted (specifically the '''31st parallel vs. the 32° 28' line''') allowed Loyalist families to rehabilitate their social standing as pioneering planters by the time the region was annexed by the United States. They became respectable Alabama planters rather than British pensioners.

=== Historical Significance ===

Moses Moore's life illustrates the pragmatic choices available to Loyalists after the Revolution. Rather than seeking vindication or compensation from the British Crown, he recognized that the future lay in the expanding American frontier. His decision to acquire Spanish land grants in territories certain to become American proved more lucrative than the Claims Commission awards received by exiles like Nicholas Welch.

The Moore family's "notorious Tory" reputation in North Carolina was forgotten in Alabama, where they were simply early pioneers. This social rehabilitation—moving from traitors to founders—was the ultimate success of the frontier relocation strategy.
