### **Major Nicholas Welch (c. 1738–1814)**
**Life Overview and Travels**
Born in **Lancaster County, Pennsylvania**, Nicholas was the son of John Welch and Elizabeth Whiteside. By the 1760s, he followed the Great Wagon Road to **Indian Creek** in Tryon (later Lincoln) County, North Carolina.

Nicholas's life was defined by the **"Loyalist Gamble"**. After twice being imprisoned for treason by Patriots, he fled to Georgia in 1779 and was commissioned as a **Major** in the **Royal North Carolina Regiment**. He is most famous for his 1780 recruitment drive at **Ramsour’s Mill**, where he rode in a brilliant red uniform and distributed gold guineas to allure recruits.

**The Exile and Diaspora**
Following the Loyalist defeat, his wife, **Elizabeth E. Moore**, and nine children were **banished** from North Carolina in October 1780. The family's movements were extensive:
*   **1781–1783:** Lived as refugees in **East Florida** (St. Johns Town at Hester’s Bluff), where they were granted 500 acres.
*   **1784–1786:** Moved to **New Providence, Bahamas**, living in "misery and want".
*   **1785–1786:** Nicholas traveled to **London** alone to petition for losses; his children were "dispersed... among such families as could be prevailed on to take them in" during this time.
*   **Children’s Fates:** Some children remained in the islands; **Mary Welch** married and died in the Turks Islands. Others returned to the mainland, including your ancestor **Lewis** and **Margaret "Peggy" Welch**, who eventually settled in Mississippi.

**Later Years**
By 1790, Nicholas had returned to the U.S., appearing in **Orangeburg, South Carolina**. In 1794, he married **Sarah Farren** in **Philadelphia**, suggesting he may have traveled back to his native Pennsylvania briefly. He lived in **Rapides Parish, Louisiana**, around 1810 before making a final move to **Giles County, Tennessee**, where he died in December 1814.

### **Lewis Welch (b. 1780)**
**Early Life and Migration**
Lewis was born in **North Carolina** just as the Revolutionary War tore his family apart. As an infant, he would have been among the children **banished to Georgia** and later taken into exile in **Florida and the Bahamas**. His childhood was one of a "Tory refugee," moving between British outposts before the family returned to the American mainland in the late 1780s.

**Rehabilitating the Family Name**
By 1800, Lewis was in **South Carolina**, where his son John C. was born. By 1810, he had moved to the "ever-expanding colony of Welches" in **Hopkins County, Kentucky**, living next to Henry Welch.

Significantly, Lewis was the generation that **reconciled the family with the United States**. While his father fought for the King, Lewis (and his brother Zachariah) served in the **War of 1812** fighting *for* the American cause.

**Tennessee Settlement**
By 1820, Lewis had moved his family to **Perry County, Tennessee**. The **1820 Census** shows his household included his sons **John C.**, **Elijah C.**, and one unknown son. His life in Tennessee was that of a foundational pioneer, helping establish the Welch lineage as a dominant administrative force in the Buffalo River valley.

### **Elijah C. Welch (1810–1867)**
**Frontier Birth and Upbringing**
Elijah was born in **Kentucky** (likely Hopkins County) during his parents' westward migration. He was brought to the Tennessee frontier as a young child. By the age of ten, he was living in **Perry County** in the household of his father, Lewis.

**Adult Life and Family**
Elijah married **Mary Taylor** (1801–1880). He became a settled farmer in the **Wayne and Perry County** region. In the **1850 Census**, he is recorded as "**Elijah C. Welsh**," age 40, born in Kentucky.

**Legacy**
His daughter **Serena Welch** married **James Monroe Hargrove**, a union that symbolized the complete integration of the once-Loyalist Welch line with a prominent Patriot Revolutionary family. Elijah died in **1867**, and his widow Mary later appeared in the Hargrove household under the name **Mary West**, suggesting a possible brief remarriage late in life.

*   **The "Crockett Dockets":** To find more on Lewis and Elijah, search for the ***Minute Book, Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions, Lawrence County, TN, 1818–1822*** (locally called the "Crockett Docket"), which contains roughly 50 mentions of the Welch family during their arrival in the region.
