# Alabama Fever
## A Song of the Kentucky-to-Alabama Migration (1816-1820)

**For Mary Kutter**

---

### Verse 1
There was a time back in old Kentucky  
When the tobacco fields done turned to dust  
Folks heard tell of Alabama cotton  
Rich black soil, if you had the guts  

They called it "Alabama Fever"  
Spread like wildfire through the hills  
From Lincoln County down to Pulaski  
Everybody caught the cotton chills

Government's making treaties every season  
Pushing borders further west  
We'll be waiting at the new-drawn line  
First to claim, first to rest

### Verse 3
From Kentucky through Tennessee borderlands  
Wagons rolling day and night  
Through the Cumberland, through the valleys  
Chasing cotton-farming dreams so bright  

Muscle Shoals, they call it Muscle Shoals  
Where the river roars and bends  
White water singing ancient stories  
Of beginnings and of ends  

### Bridge
The river sings, oh the river sings  
Of all who came before  
The river sings, oh the river sings  
But we don't hear no more  

We got Alabama Fever  
Nothing else will do  
Fresh land, cotton prices rising  
We're starting something new  

### Verse 4
Valentine Hargrove left his Kentucky home  
Joseph Lewis and the Whitley clan  
Crossed the Tennessee state line together  
Every woman, child, and man  

Left behind the worn-out pastures  
Left behind the rocky ground  
Headed for the Alabama Territory  
Where the cotton grows year-round

Alabama Fever, Alabama Fever  
Burning in our bones  
Down to the Shoals where the river sings  
We're gonna make our home  
Alabama Fever, Alabama Fever

'Bout everything we lost

Through the Muscle Shoals so strong  
Singing 'bout the ones who had it first

## Historical Context

**Alabama Fever** was the term used to describe the mass migration from Kentucky, Tennessee, Georgia, and the Carolinas to the Alabama Territory between 1816 and 1820. The movement was driven by:

- **Exhausted tobacco lands** in Kentucky and Virginia
- **Cotton boom** following the invention of the cotton gin
- **Cheap, fertile land** in the Alabama Territory
- **Creek land cessions** after the Creek War (1813-1814)
- **Cherokee land cessions** throughout the 1810s-1830s

### The Muscle Shoals Region

The **Muscle Shoals** area along the Tennessee River was particularly desirable:
- Located in northwest Alabama
- The Tennessee River "sings" through the rocky shoals
- Extremely fertile bottomlands
- Strategic location for river transport

### Key Families in This Migration

From the genealogy files, several families caught "Alabama Fever":

- **[[Hargrove-277|Valentine Hargrove]]** (1778-1858) - Moved from Kentucky to Tennessee/Alabama borderlands
- **[[Lewis-8883|Ruth Whitley Lewis]]** - Married Valentine; daughter of Kentucky pioneers
- **[[Whitley-757|William Whitley]]** family - Kentucky pioneers whose descendants moved south
- **[[Lewis-9841|Joseph Francis Lewis]]** - Part of the Kentucky-to-Alabama movement
- **England, Welch, Brewer families** - All participated in the southward migration

### The Reality of "Renting Indian Lands"

White settlers often **illegally squatted** on Creek and Cherokee lands before official cessions, or rented small plots from Native landholders while waiting for government removal policies. This was:
- **Illegal under federal law** but rarely enforced
- **Temporary arrangement** - settlers knew removal was coming
- **Part of pressure tactics** to force Native nations to cede more land

The song reflects the settlers' perspective but acknowledges in the bridge and outro that they were taking land from peoples who were there first. The river "singing" represents the memory and perspective that the settlers ignored in their fever to claim new land.

## Musical Notes

**Suggested style:** Appalachian folk, mountain ballad  
**Tempo:** Moderate, driving rhythm (like a wagon rolling)  
**Key:** D minor or A minor (for a somewhat melancholy, historical feel)  
**Instrumentation:** Fiddle, banjo, acoustic guitar, upright bass, possibly dulcimer

The "river that sings" refers to the **Tennessee River at Muscle Shoals**, where water rushing over rocky shoals creates a distinctive sound that Native peoples and early settlers described as singing or roaring.
